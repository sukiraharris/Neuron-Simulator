A neuron on your screen

Three live simulations of how brain cells fire, from a single cell up to a thousand. Everything here is computed in your browser, straight from the equations.

By Sukira Harris

One neuron, the Hodgkin–Huxley way

In 1952, Alan Hodgkin and Andrew Huxley worked out how a nerve cell fires, work that won them the Nobel Prize. Sodium rushes in and the voltage shoots up. Potassium flows out and pulls it back down. That up-and-down is a spike.

Push current into the cell with the slider. Below about 6, it stays quiet. Above that, it fires on its own, and more current means faster firing.

Try lowering sodium until the spikes vanish, or set current to zero and give the cell a single jolt.

Different cells, different rhythms

Real neurons don't all fire the same way. Eugene Izhikevich found a two-equation model that can imitate many cell types just by changing four numbers.

Pick a cell type. The current switches on partway through, so you can see how each one reacts.

Compare how regular spiking slows down over time while fast spiking never tires.

A thousand neurons talking

Here are 800 excitatory cells, which make their neighbors more likely to fire, and 200 inhibitory cells, which calm them down. Each one also gets random input, like background chatter from the rest of the brain.

Every dot is one spike. Each row is a cell, and time runs left to right. The vertical stripes are groups of cells firing together, rhythms like the alpha and gamma waves measured in real brains.

Turn the connections down to zero and watch the rhythm dissolve into scattered noise.
